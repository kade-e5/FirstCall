BOPIM: The Blood Or Other Potentially Infectious Materials Standard

The OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens Standard (29 CFR 1910.1030), established in 1991, safeguards employees who may encounter blood or other potentially infectious materials (commonly referred to as OPIM, though sometimes abbreviated in contexts as BOPIM) during their work. These materials can harbor serious pathogens, including Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, HIV, and various other infectious agents capable of causing disease.

This regulation protects an estimated 5.6 million workers across healthcare fields, related professions, and industries involving potential exposure—such as biohazard remediation and crime scene cleanup companies—by mandating measures like exposure control plans, proper use of personal protective equipment, engineering controls, employee training, hepatitis B vaccination offerings, and safe handling/disposal procedures to minimize occupational risks from these hazards.

The OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens Standard (29 CFR 1910.1030) imposes several key obligations on employers to minimize occupational exposure risks. These include:

  • Safe handling, transport, and disposal of biohazardous/medical waste
  • Proper use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)
  • Work practice controls to reduce exposure
  • Exposure Control Plan with risk assessments
  • Accurate recordkeeping
  • Mandatory initial and annual training
  • Free hepatitis B vaccination for at-risk employees

Do You Need Special Certification for Biohazard Remediation in Durango, CO?

No formal certification or license is typically mandated at the federal level to become a biohazard cleanup technician. That said, anyone handling materials that could contain bloodborne pathogens must strictly follow OSHA safety protocols. This includes using appropriate personal protective equipment at all times. In Colorado, as in most states, there are no unique statewide licensing rules specifically for trauma or crime scene remediation—but it's essential to review any applicable city, county, state, or federal regulations. These could involve waste disposal guidelines, background screening, or other compliance steps that apply to companies in this field.

Personal protective equipment (PPE) serves as the critical final barrier protecting workers from dangerous contaminants. It shields vulnerable areas like the skin, eyes, face, and respiratory system from exposure to biohazards. The specific gear required can vary depending on the site's conditions and the nature of the contaminants involved. At minimum, technicians should always wear full-face shields or goggles, puncture-resistant nitrile gloves, fluid-impervious coveralls or suits, and an appropriate respirator rated for the hazards present.

We prioritize comprehensive training for our teams, especially newcomers entering biohazard work. This instruction covers essential on-the-job safety practices aligned with OSHA standards, including proper techniques for managing and disposing of hazardous biological waste. Mastering these protocols significantly lowers the risk of incidents, exposure mishaps, or legal complications down the line.

First Calls Formula for Biohazard Cleanup:

  1. Our certified team begins every biohazard project with a thorough on-site evaluation. We carefully identify and mark all contaminated zones, conduct a detailed risk and safety review, and assess the full scope of the hazard. Once a clear, customized remediation strategy is developed, our specialists move forward with immediate action to contain and address the issue.
  2. From the outset, all crew members implement strict safety protocols to protect themselves, occupants, and bystanders from any exposure risks in affected areas.
  3. The team systematically extracts and securely disposes of all items or substances that could harbor infectious agents, ensuring nothing hazardous remains on your property.
  4. Federal OSHA regulations (under the Bloodborne Pathogens Standard) provide precise instructions for managing potentially infectious waste. For instance, fluids such as cerebrospinal, peritoneal, amniotic, or other bodily substances are collected with extreme care and transported to approved, licensed facilities for proper incineration or treatment.
  5. In line with industry best practices and regulatory guidelines, any absorbent or porous surfaces—like carpets, upholstery, fabrics, or drywall—that cannot be fully decontaminated are completely removed and discarded to eliminate ongoing health risks.
  6. Durable, non-absorbent items that can be effectively restored undergo rigorous cleaning using powerful, hospital-grade EPA-registered disinfectants. If any non-porous elements prove impossible to sanitize safely, our experts will remove and dispose of them responsibly.
  7. After thorough cleaning, we apply specialized deodorization treatments throughout the impacted spaces to eliminate lingering smells and restore them to a safe, pre-incident state. To confirm complete remediation and readiness for re-entry, we often recommend (or arrange) independent clearance testing by a qualified industrial hygienist, who provides documented verification that contamination levels meet health and safety standards.
  8. In most cases, removing unsalvageable contaminated items combined with hospital-grade disinfection effectively resolves odors. For more persistent or complex situations, we deploy additional professional deodorization techniques—such as thermal fogging, ozone treatment, or hydroxyl generators—to achieve full success and a fresh environment.
